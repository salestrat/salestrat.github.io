AI in marketing: what current research actually says

The trend is real, but the useful story is about work and customer expectations—not hype. Salesforce's 2026 State of Marketing surveyed 4,450 marketing professionals across 26 countries. In that survey, 69% said they struggle to respond promptly to customer and prospect enquiries, and 83% said customers increasingly want two-way conversations. The report also found 88% of marketers are optimizing for AI-driven search experiences. These are survey findings, not proof that any one AI tool will improve a specific company's results.

There is also evidence that a well-scoped assistant can help staff do certain work faster. A study of 5,179 customer-support agents found that generative AI assistance increased issues resolved per hour by 14% on average, with larger gains among novice and lower-skilled agents. That is a customer-support result from one setting—not a promise of a 14% sales lift or a forecast for every small business. Read the NBER research paper for the study design and limits.

Put together, the market case is practical: customers want timely, useful responses; teams are stretched across channels; and AI can support selected tasks. A competitive advantage comes from fitting it to your process and data, then checking whether customer outcomes improve.

How AI can reduce marketing costs—and how to calculate the real value

AI may reduce costs when it removes avoidable time from research, content production, reporting, lead handling, or other recurring work—or helps a team use its existing tools and budget more effectively. It does not make strategy, software, quality checks, integration, or customer support free. For many businesses, the first benefit is capacity: people spend less time on repetitive tasks and more time improving the customer experience and the offer.

A simple monthly modelNet value = value of time saved + incremental gross profit − total AI operating cost
  • Count tool and message fees, integration, maintenance, and human review.
  • Measure staff time saved on the specific workflow AI actually supports.
  • Attribute revenue only when a tested, measurable contribution is supported by the data.

Track the measures tied to the use case: hours per campaign brief, time to publish approved content, cost per qualified lead, cost per resolved enquiry, campaign conversion, error or rework rate, and customer satisfaction. Compare with a baseline before launch. Do not count generated drafts, chatbot interactions, or attributed clicks as savings or revenue by themselves.

A practical AI implementation roadmap

  1. Choose a measurable business problem. Examples include slow campaign production, inconsistent brand adaptation, manual reporting, unanswered customer questions, or fragmented follow-up.
  2. Map the workflow and baseline. Document the current tools, time, costs, quality checks, customer impact, and success measure before selecting an AI solution.
  3. Prepare trusted information. Organize approved brand guidance, product or service details, policies, performance data, and source material; resolve outdated or contradictory information.
  4. Select proportionate tools and integrations. Use the least complex setup that meets the need. Connect a CRM, store, analytics platform, or messaging channel only where there is a clear purpose and appropriate permission.
  5. Define human review and data boundaries. Specify what AI can draft, summarize, recommend, or answer; what needs approval; what it must not do; and how personal information is protected.
  6. Test representative situations. Check everyday cases, edge cases, inaccurate inputs, failure paths, and handoffs. Verify output quality and the impact on existing systems before launch.
  7. Pilot, measure, and improve. Start with a limited workflow, compare its outcomes with the baseline, gather customer and team feedback, and expand only when quality and economics support it.

Trust is part of the conversion experience

A good AI assistant is transparent, useful, and easy to leave. Tell visitors when they are chatting with AI. Ask only for information needed for the next step, explain how it will be used, limit access, and follow applicable privacy and messaging rules. Avoid putting sensitive customer information into tools that are not approved for it.

Set a clear human route for complaints, exceptions, high-value opportunities, uncertain answers, and requests to speak to a person. Keep a human owner responsible for the knowledge base and audit conversations for accuracy, bias, unsafe recommendations, and broken handoffs. AI should support your people, not quietly make consequential decisions without review.
